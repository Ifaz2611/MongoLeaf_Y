    02 — The field guide

    Eight conditions,
    one scan away.

    Trained on eight critical mango orchard conditions. Review field identification signs, high-risk weather triggers, cultural sanitation, and proven treatment dosages.

    • Healthy

      Healthy leaf

      Mangifera indica (Reference)

      Uniform deep green, glossy surface with intact cuticle and zero lesions, spots, curling, or fungal growth.

      Part: All canopy foliage & panicles
      Goal: Optimal photosynthesis & tree vigor
      Farmer Field Advisory
      Weekly Scouting: Inspect 20 random leaves per tree once a week across all 4 quadrants of the canopy to catch early spots.
      Soil & Nutrition: Apply 30–50 kg aged Farm Yard Manure (FYM) per mature tree post-harvest. Spray Zinc (0.2%) + Borax (0.1%) during new vegetative flush.
      Irrigation Hygiene: Irrigate deeply along the root drip-line; never wet canopy foliage with overhead sprinklers.
      Keep monitoring new flush & pre-bloom
    • Fungal

      Anthracnose

      Colletotrichum gloeosporioides

      Dark, sunken spots on leaves, twigs and fruit; lesions can merge into larger patches and cause severe blossom blight.

      Part: Tender leaves, panicles, twigs, fruit
      Trigger: Warm & wet weather (24–30°C, RH >80%)
      Farmer Field Advisory
      Visual Clues: Angular dark brown spots that dry and crack into "shot-holes"; flower panicles turn black and shrivel within 48h of rain.
      Sanitation: Prune dead twigs 5–10 cm into green wood; rake and burn fallen leaves and mummified fruit on the orchard floor.
      Chemical Spray & Dosage: Copper Oxychloride 50 WP @ 2.5–3.0 g/L or Mancozeb 75 WP @ 2.0 g/L preventive. Curative: Carbendazim 50 WP @ 1.0 g/L or Azoxystrobin @ 1.0 ml/L.
      Pollinator Note: Never spray during full bloom to protect honeybees and fruit set.
      Thrives in warm, wet weather & rain splash
    • Bacterial

      Bacterial Canker

      Xanthomonas citri pv. mangiferaeindicae

      Raised, corky lesions often ringed by a yellow halo; causes gummy exudation, shoot die-off, and fruit drop.

      Part: Leaves, young twigs, fruit skin
      Trigger: Windstorms, hail injuries, rain splash (25–32°C)
      Farmer Field Advisory
      Visual Clues: Water-soaked angular dark spots surrounded by a bright yellow halo; raised, rough crusts; gummy sap oozing from twigs.
      Sanitation & Tools: Prune shoots only during dry weather. Dip secateurs in 10% household bleach or 70% alcohol between trees.
      Bactericide Spray: Streptocycline (or Streptomycin) @ 0.15–0.2 g/L combined with Copper Oxychloride 50 WP @ 2.5 g/L at 15-day intervals.
      Organic Alternative: Copper hydroxide @ 2.0 g/L sprayed twice during early vegetative shoot flush.
      Spreads with rain splash & unsterilized shears
    • Pest

      Cutting Weevil

      Deporaus marginatus

      Notched, ragged leaf edges where adult weevils feed; tender new leaves cleanly severed straight across like scissors.

      Part: Tender new flush & young leaves
      Trigger: Vegetative flush emergence; active at dusk/night
      Farmer Field Advisory
      Visual Clues: Leaves cleanly severed across the blade; severed leaf tips littering the ground below the canopy containing weevil eggs.
      Sanitation & Tillage: Collect and burn or bury fallen severed leaves daily to kill eggs before larvae burrow into soil. Lightly till basin soil.
      Targeted Control: Spray 5% Neem Seed Kernel Extract (NSKE) or Spinosad 45 SC @ 0.3 ml/L or Chlorpyrifos 20 EC @ 2.0 ml/L at first flush sign.
      Monitoring: Inspect newly flushed trees at twilight with a flashlight when adult weevils feed actively.
      Look for severed leaves on ground & nocturnal feeding
    • Fungal

      Die Back

      Lasiodiplodia theobromae

      Shoots brown and wither from the tip downward; dead brown leaves remain stubbornly attached to dry, brittle twigs.

      Part: Terminal twigs, branches, vascular tissue
      Trigger: Drought stress, waterlogging, unsealed pruning cuts
      Farmer Field Advisory
      Visual Clues: Downward branch browning; dead leaves stay attached rather than dropping; dark discoloration inside split twigs.
      Deep Pruning: Prune affected twigs 7–10 cm below the visible dead margin into healthy green wood. Burn all pruned branches at once.
      Cut Dressing: Immediately apply a thick Copper Oxychloride paste (1:1 with water or linseed oil) on all cut ends thicker than a finger.
      Foliar Spray: Thiophanate-methyl 70 WP @ 1.0 g/L or Carbendazim 50 WP @ 1.0 g/L sprayed twice post-pruning.
      Often follows stress, drought or unsealed pruning wounds
    • Pest

      Gall Midge

      Procontarinia matteiana

      Small, blister-like wart galls on leaves and new shoots; heavy galling causes leaves to curl, twist, and drop early.

      Part: Tender new leaves & growing shoots
      Trigger: Flush emergence; still, warm and humid weather
      Farmer Field Advisory
      Visual Clues: Dense raised pimples on leaf blade; distorted stunted growth; tiny exit pinholes on older dried blisters.
      Sanitation: Prune and destroy heavily galled shoots before adult midges emerge. Preserve native parasitic wasps.
      Soil Cultivation: Lightly till orchard tree basins to bury pupating larvae and expose them to predatory birds and sun.
      Insecticide Spray: Spray 5% NSKE or Imidacloprid 17.8 SL @ 0.3 ml/L or Dimethoate 30 EC @ 1.5 ml/L at first flush emergence.
      Check tender new flush & leaf undersides
    • Fungal

      Powdery Mildew

      Oidium mangiferae

      White, powdery coating on leaf surface, shoots and flower panicles; causes flowers to dry up and drop without fruit set.

      Part: Inflorescences (panicles), tender leaves, pea fruit
      Trigger: Cool nights (10–14°C) & warm days (27–31°C) during bloom
      Farmer Field Advisory
      Visual Clues: White flour-like dust on panicles and young leaves; blossoms turn purple-brown and drop; leaves curl and turn leathery.
      Canopy Aeration: Prune dense inner branches to eliminate shaded, humid air pockets inside the tree crown.
      Protective Sprays: Wettable Sulfur 80 WP @ 2.0–2.5 g/L at panicle emergence. Follow with Hexaconazole 5 EC @ 1.0 ml/L or Dinocap @ 1.0 ml/L at bud break.
      Temperature Warning: Never spray sulfur when temperatures exceed 32°C to prevent sulfur chemical scorch.
      Improve airflow through canopy & avoid shade pockets
    • Fungal

      Sooty Mould

      Capnodium mangiferae / Meliola spp.

      Black, sooty film on leaves and twigs, growing on sticky honeydew left behind by sap-sucking mango hoppers and scales.

      Part: Upper leaf surfaces, twigs, fruit skin
      Trigger: Secondary to sap-sucking insect infestations
      Farmer Field Advisory
      Visual Clues: Velvety black layer that easily rubs off with your thumb; leaf under the layer is green but deprived of sunlight.
      Starch Peel Wash: Spray 1–2% boiled starch solution (boiled maida or rice starch @ 15 g/L). In dry sunlight it flakes off, peeling the black mould!
      Target the Insects: Eradicate honeydew-secreting hoppers with Neem oil (5 ml/L) or Thiamethoxam 25 WG @ 0.2 g/L or Imidacloprid @ 0.3 ml/L.
      Canopy Wash: Follow with a mild copper or wettable sulfur wash to clean remaining residue.
      Treat the sucking insects, not just the surface mould

    Seasonal Orchard Schedule

    Year-round growth stages & spray timing

    Disease prevention in mango orchards depends strictly on seasonal timing. Aligning your scouting and protective measures with tree phenology prevents 90% of yield losses.

    Stage 01 · Post-Harvest

    Dormancy & Sanitation

    August – October
    • Orchard action: Prune criss-cross, dead, and diseased twigs back 10 cm into green wood. Rake, burn or deep-bury fallen leaves and mummies.
    • Protective spray: Bordeaux mixture (1%) or Copper Oxychloride 50 WP @ 3.0 g/L immediately after pruning to sterilize wounds.
    • Nutrition: Apply well-rotted FYM (30–50 kg/tree) and basal phosphate/potash around the drip line.
    Stage 02 · Vegetative Flush

    New Shoot Protection

    October – December
    • Orchard action: Scout tender copper-red flushes for Cutting Weevil leaf cuts and Gall Midge blisters. Collect severed leaves.
    • Protective spray: 5% Neem Seed Kernel Extract (NSKE) or Spinosad 45 SC @ 0.3 ml/L on tender flushes.
    • Disease alert: Spray Mancozeb 75 WP @ 2.0 g/L if rainy periods occur to stop Anthracnose leaf spots.
    Stage 03 · Panicle Flush

    Blossom Protection

    December – February
    • Orchard action: Watch for blossom blights. Powdery mildew and Anthracnose can destroy 80% of bloom in 48 hours of cloudy weather.
    • Protective spray: Wettable Sulfur 80 WP @ 2.0 g/L or Hexaconazole 5 EC @ 1.0 ml/L right before blossoms open.
    • Watering: Minimize irrigation during flower bud induction to prevent vegetative shoots replacing flower panicles.
    Stage 04 · Full Bloom

    Pollinator Refuge (Zero Spray)

    February – March
    • CRITICAL RULE: STRICTLY NO INSECTICIDE SPRAYS! Mango blossoms require honeybees, wild stingless bees, and syrphids for pollination.
    • Orchard action: Protect pollinator populations. Keep honeybee boxes in the orchard (2–3 boxes/acre).
    • Emergency mildew: If severe powdery mildew hits during bloom, use only gentle biological spray late in the evening.
    Stage 05 · Fruit Set

    Pea to Marble Stage

    March – May
    • Orchard action: Guard against premature fruit drop, Anthracnose tear-stains, and Bacterial Canker fruit cracking.
    • Protective spray: Copper Hydroxide @ 2.0 g/L or Mancozeb 75 WP @ 2.0 g/L after fruit set. Add Boron (0.1%) to strengthen skin.
    • Irrigation: Resume regular light irrigation under the canopy drip-line; maintain 10 cm organic straw mulch.
    Stage 06 · Pre-Harvest

    Maturation & Safe Harvest

    May – July
    • Orchard action: Install Methyl Eugenol pheromone traps (10 traps/ha) for fruit flies; bag fruit for export quality.
    • Pre-Harvest Interval (PHI): Stop ALL chemical fungicide and pesticide sprays at least 15–21 days prior to picking fruit.
    • Harvest hygiene: Harvest with 1 cm stem attached to prevent latex burn; wash fruits in warm water (52°C for 5 min) against post-harvest rot.

    Good Agricultural Practices

    The 5 golden rules for mango growers

    Simple agronomic habits prevent outbreaks before they start, save thousands on chemical sprays, and protect fruit quality.

    01

    Orchard Floor Sanitation

    The 70% Rule: Over 70% of Anthracnose fungal spores and overwintering weevil pupae survive in fallen leaves, weeds, and mummified fruits on the ground. Rake the orchard floor every 2 weeks. Burn or deep-bury (>40 cm) infected debris outside the orchard.

    02

    Tool Sterilization Between Trees

    Prevent Mechanical Transmission: Bacterial Canker (Xanthomonas) and Die Back (Lasiodiplodia) are easily carried from sick trees to healthy trees on secateurs and saws. Dip blades in 10% bleach or 70% alcohol between trees. Paint cuts >2 cm with Copper paste.

    03

    Spray Timing & Weather Discipline

    Avoid Heat & Rain Loss: Always spray during calm early mornings (6:00–9:00 AM) or late afternoons (4:30–6:30 PM). Never spray in midday heat above 32°C (causes leaf scorch/phytotoxicity) or when rain is expected within 4 hours. Ensure underside leaf coverage.

    04

    Fungicide Rotation (FRAC Codes)

    Stop Resistance Build-up: Never spray single-site systemic fungicides (like Carbendazim or Hexaconazole) more than two times consecutively. Always rotate with multi-site contact protectants like Copper Oxychloride or Mancozeb to prevent resistant fungal strains.

    05

    Canopy Sunlight & Root Drip Watering

    Keep Foliage Dry: Fungi and bacteria require a film of standing water on the leaf surface to penetrate. Never use overhead sprinklers that wet the canopy. Use drip irrigation around the tree drip-line. Center-prune water shoots annually so sunlight reaches inner branches.

    A note on the results

    Useful guidance, not a lab report.

    A photo only captures so much. Lighting, angle and leaf age can all sway a prediction. Treat the diagnosis as a strong first opinion — and confirm anything unusual with your local agricultural extension officer before applying chemicals.
